Many tasks offer penalties for suboptimal conditions. Looking these up and assessing them can be time-consuming, however, while Quick-and-Dirty Modifiers (p. 570) may be too quick and dirty.

As an alternative, the GM can set a single difficulty – the Basic Abstract Difficulty (BAD) – covering all aspects of a particular phase of the adventure. This penalty from 0 to -10 replaces detailed situational modifiers. The only other modifiers that apply are those the PCs bring into the picture: equipment bonuses, disadvantage penalties, etc.

**Example:** The heroes are infiltrating a secret base. The GM sets BAD at -5. Rolls to climb walls, pick locks, disarm alarms, etc., are thus at -5 instead of detailed modifiers for things like security-system quality. If someone’s equipment gives +2, that modifier still applies.

When the PCs meet important opponents without character sheets, treat those NPCs’ effective skill as 10 + absolute value of BAD: 11 at -1, 12 at -2, and so on. This abstracts actual skill, equipment quality, extra time, tactics, etc. – don’t modify explicitly for these things. In Contests against this skill level, BAD doesn’t penalize the PCs’ rolls (it’s already accounted for).

The GM can pick whatever BAD “feels right.” When facing a known enemy group, consider the adversaries’ basic point value as an Enemy (p. 135), divided by 4, dropping fractions. Regardless, BAD can change during an adventure! It traditionally worsens as the heroes approach the conclusion. Bad plans or choices – or critical failures – might also make it worse, but then exceptionally good ideas or rolls should do the opposite.

The GM can revert to quick-and-dirty or detailed modifiers – and NPCs with character sheets – whenever that seems fun. The goal of BAD is to save time, not to supplant the GM’s judgment.
