A Talent’s reaction bonus (p. 89) assumes that observers find it pleasing to witness the gifted use of the skills involved. A master can show off more than a beginner, regardless of Talent – but a talented person seems inspired regardless of skill level. Yet one could argue that showing off is more impressive than showing aptitude, or that a high controlling attribute makes one as much of a “natural” as high Talent. This calls into question a reaction bonus for talented individuals but not for masters or the broadly gifted.

Removing the reaction bonus would be unfair – it’s a significant part of what the price of Talent buys. Replacing it is another matter! Talent might give another edge to people who intuitively grasp the affected skills so that, for instance, IQ 11 with Animal Friend 3 means “animals like you,” while IQ 14 means “smart, which helps you figure out animals (and people, plants, machines, taxes, etc.).” An incomplete list of alternatives:

- A bonus to use suitable Influence skills (p. 359) in situations where they’d benefit from a show of Talent. After successfully using one of the Talent’s skills in front of others, the Talent aids an appropriate Influence roll on those people, producing effects similar to a reaction bonus.

- A bonus to follow-up rolls triggered by applying the Talent’s skills. For example, a Talent that covers Running might aid basic HT rolls to avoid crippling due to critical failure at extra effort with that skill, while one that covers Speed-Reading could help IQ rolls to recall what was read.

- A bonus to default rolls – much as for Jack of All Trades (p. 327) – for skills that one of the Talent’s skills regularly pulls in but that the Talent doesn’t encompass.

- A bonus to rolls related to the Talent’s theme but for which there are no skills. For instance, if a Talent’s premise is “good with gadgets,” it may help rolls on the Enigmatic Device Table (pp. 478-479), while if the Talent’s concept is “finding clues,” it’s fair to add it to Per rolls to notice things that none of its skills look for. If the roll in question isn’t made by the character but against them, the Talent should penalize it instead.

- A bonus in Contests against skills the Talent covers, when these are pitted against the talented individual. This is valid only if some of those skills precipitate Contests; e.g., Influence skills, which are resisted by Will, but not Forced Entry, which works only on inanimate objects. Boosted resistance to diametrically opposed skills is also fitting.

- A bonus to rolls to use conceptually related advantages; e.g., Healing for Healer. The Talent acts much like a power Talent (see Other Powers, p. 257), but aids advantages on the basis of theme rather than power modifier (p. 331).

- A bonus similar to any of the above for somebody else, if the Talent is “selfless”; e.g., Healer might give a bonus to the HT rolls of patients the talented medic treats.

- An offset against penalties, especially those under Equipment Modifiers (p. 345), Familiarity (p. 169), Geographical and Temporal Scope (p. 176), and Tech-Level Modifiers (p. 168). This is fairest when it benefits skills other than the ones the Talent aids, after using the Talent’s skills to set up those tasks, because improving the Talent’s skills and removing penalties to them is double-dipping.

- Access to a feat that the untalented can’t attempt – in the spirit of the Per roll for Danger Sense (p. 47) or the IQ roll for Empathy (p. 51). Consider a roll against attribute-4 plus Talent level, so that someone with the maximum four levels makes a straight attribute roll.

The GM may impose whatever restrictions seem necessary to preserve the Talent’s theme and flavor. The resulting effects should be roughly in line with situational attribute bonuses such as Acute Senses (p. 35), Fearlessness (p. 55), and Hard to Subdue (p. 59). This is art, not science! The more expensive the Talent, the more substantial its benefits can be without becoming unbalanced. A Talent that encompasses rarely used skills may need a meaty bonus to be attractive, while one benefiting “must have” skills will be popular even without extras.

“No extra benefit” is sometimes fair. If a Talent already aids many valuable skills, it needs no further bonuses to be balanced. If Talent grants no reaction bonus in the first place, there’s nothing to replace!

And sometimes, a reaction bonus is the best non-skill benefit. A Talent may represent a gift that a particular group can sense even in the unskilled – pheromones, a mark on the soul, an intuitive sense of respect, whatever. If so, a reaction bonus is the most reasonable choice.

The GM has the final say on what benefits – if any – each Talent grants.
