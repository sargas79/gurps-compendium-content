It often makes sense to define several abilities as distinct, mutually exclusive “settings” of a single, adaptable trait. For instance, Burning Attack (p. 61), Ladar (p. 81), and Laser Communication (p. 91) might be different intensity levels and modulations for a laser that can function as a weapon, or a sense, or a means of communication. Similarly, different Innate Attacks might represent a ray gun’s power settings.

If only one such ability works at a time, a cost break is reasonable. If the GM agrees, characters with “alternative abilities” pay full price for only their most expensive ability. The rest are at 1/5 cost. Find each ability’s cost after all enhancements and limitations, apply the divisor, and round up.

Example: Jocko the psychokinetic has Flight (Psychokinetic, -10%) [36], Super Jump 2 (Psychokinetic, -10%) [18], and Walk on Air (Psychokinetic, -10%) [18], defined as a single ability to levitate. Jocko pays full price for the most expensive ability, Flight. The other two are at 1/5 cost; each costs 18/5 = 3.6 points, rounded up to 4 points. Final cost is 36 + 4 + 4 = 44 points.

Alternatives needn’t be limited to one ability at a time. To be able to use any two at a time, pay full price for the two most expensive abilities and 1/5 cost for the rest; to use three at a time, pay full price for the three most expensive ones; and so on. Think of this as providing a number of “slots,” each of which one of the alternative abilities can fill. A given ability can fill only one slot at a time.

Example: Misty buys DR 10, Sealed, and Slippery 10 as alternative abilities with two slots. She can have any two of the three “on” at once. She cannot load DR 10 into both slots for a total DR 20!

The GM may forbid abilities with incompatible in-game explanations as alternatives. Obscure and Burning Attack defined as smoke and a fireball are reasonable alternatives . . . but make the smoke darkness and the fireball a laser, and the combination would need superscience or the supernatural to make sense!

Alternative abilities have three drawbacks:

1. The number of abilities you can use simultaneously is limited to the number you paid full price for, and it’s impossible to have a Link (p. 106) between them. To change out a slot requires a Ready maneuver. After switching to an attack (Affliction, Binding, Innate Attack, Neutralize, Obscure, Psi Static, and anything else the GM deems an “attack”), switching to a different attack in that slot is a free action. If the user can make multiple attacks per turn, they must use the same setting for all of them.

2. Anything that disables one ability disables the whole collection, regardless of the number of slots. If injury or critical failure cripples or burns out any of the abilities – or a foe neutralizes or drains one – none of them are available until the affected one recovers!

3. Using an ability that can’t be activated again until its duration expires (e.g., Luck, or almost anything with the Ranged enhancement) effectively “freezes” that slot – the ability cannot be swapped out of it until the duration ends. You may continue to swap other abilities into remaining slots. If you have just one slot, your entire alternative ability array is frozen for the duration.

This rule generalizes Alternative Attacks (p. 61), which has additional notes for attacks.
