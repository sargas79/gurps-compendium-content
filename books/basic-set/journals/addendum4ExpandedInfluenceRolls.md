Accounting for modifiers, a reaction roll can produce a wide range of outcomes, from “Disastrous” to “Excellent” (pp. 560-561), whereas the result of an Influence roll (p. 359) is binary – normally either “Bad” or “Good.” As an optional rule, Influence rolls may generate a wider range of reactions, based on the margin of victory or loss in the Quick Contest. Use this table to convert margin to reaction:

| Margin | Reaction |
| --- | --- |
| Victory by 8 or more\* | Excellent |
| Victory by 5-7 | Very Good |
| Victory by 1-4 | Good |
| Tie | Neutral |
| Loss by 1-2 | Poor |
| Loss by 3-4 | Bad |
| Loss by 5-7 | Very Bad |
| Loss by 8 or more† | Disastrous |

\* Or, at the GM’s option, if the influencer rolls a critical success and the subject does not.

† Or, at the GM’s option, if the influencer rolls a critical failure and the subject does not.

Don’t use this table if some other skill roll dictates automatic failure of the Influence roll, as with the automatic “Very Bad” reaction for losing the preliminary Quick Contest for specious intimidation (p. 202).

This rule alters one of the trade-offs between reaction modifiers and Influence skills: Under the standard rules, Influence skills give predictable, moderately useful results; reaction rolls can give more dramatic results, but the outcomes are less consistent. With this rule, the big difference is that a strong-willed person can resist Influence skills better, but remains susceptible to reaction modifiers.
